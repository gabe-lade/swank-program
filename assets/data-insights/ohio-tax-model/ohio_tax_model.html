What this is. This is a short-run model of what would happen if Ohio's property taxes went away. You decide what replaces them and how much. It estimates how the tax burden would shift among Ohio households and, if the money is not fully replaced, which levels of government and which services would be most exposed.

      How we calculate this

      What this does not include

      • Anything beyond the first year. Households and businesses can change what they buy, where they shop and where they live.
      • Where taxes are collected versus where people live. Sales tax is counted where people shop, so money raised in a county is not the same as what that county's residents pay.
      • Changes in home prices. Property-tax changes are partly built into home prices, so the first-year change in a bill is not the same as who gains in the end (Oates 1969; Cellini, Ferreira and Rothstein 2010).
      • Farms and farm land on their own. "Other homes and land," under "Where the money goes," is what is left of residential and farm property tax after the bills homeowners report, small rental buildings and the state's credits are taken out. It holds farm land, vacant land, second homes and property whose owners live elsewhere. It is not a measured farm total, and some farm property tax is inside the bills farm households report. None of that remainder is added to any household's bar, so households that own that land may be better off than the charts show. The Current Agricultural Use Value (CAUV) program is not modeled.
      • What lawmakers or voters would do. This tool does not forecast it.
      • The tax on business equipment. This page covers real property tax only.

      The household averages are the project's published household estimates, built from a survey of Ohio households. Each average is for homeowners or renters in an income range. A survey average is never exact, so for each one we also measure how much it could move if we had surveyed different households. Then we choose what to print.

      • A dollar amount when the average is well pinned down (the likely error is under 30% of the average).
      • About even when the likely range includes no change at all. We show it as an open circle on the zero line.
      • A range when we are sure the household pays less (or more) but not by how much. The solid bar is our best estimate. The light band and the words "between $A and $B" (or "up to $B" when the low end rounds to $0) show the likely range: our estimate minus and plus its likely error, rounded to the nearest $10. That covers the true value about 9 times in 10.
      • Not shown when fewer than 100 households were surveyed in that group, which is too few for any estimate.

      How "Your rough estimate" is built

      • Owners: the home's market value (the county auditor's value, the one behind your tax bill), times the share of market value that is taxed, times the county's typical residential tax rate (net tax charged over taxable value, tax year 2025, before the state's credits), less the state's homeowner credits as the household averages treat them.
      • Renters: twelve months of rent, times the share of rent that is property tax, times the share landlords pass on. For a house or small building (up to 4 units) the share comes from the household model's own estimate of the property tax inside rent: a price-to-rent ratio times a residential tax rate. For an apartment building (5 or more units) it comes from the model's apartment estimate: how many years of rent such a building sells for, times the commercial and industrial tax rate. Both are rescaled by how the county's rate compares with the state's.
      • New taxes: the average added sales and income tax for the income range, owners or renters, in the county's region, at the settings you chose. Where too few survey households live in that region, the statewide average stands in.
      • Auditor values and survey values differ. The household averages rest on home values owners report in a survey, which run lower than county auditor market values. A home entered at its auditor value will usually show a larger property tax than the average for the same income range.

      Terms used on this page

      • First-year: the change in the first year, before people, businesses and home prices adjust. It is not a calendar year.
      • How much people respond: how much people change what they buy, or how much they earn or report, when a tax changes. "Typical" is our standard setting; "Strong" assumes people change by more; "None" assumes they change nothing.
      • Pass-through: the share of a landlord's property-tax change that shows up in rent.
      • JVSD: a joint vocational school district, a career-tech school district that several districts share.
      • Business property (Ohio's "Class II"): commercial, industrial and mineral property. Homes and farms are "Class I." Class II includes apartment buildings; this page shows their tax with renters and landlords, not with business property.

      • After paying for the change: after the household also pays the new taxes that replace the property tax. The number is the net change: the tax it stops paying, less the new taxes it pays.
      • Inflation Cap Credit: a school tax credit from House Bill 186 (HB 186).
